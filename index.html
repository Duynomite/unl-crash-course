Section 1

What you're selling

You're selling two distinct products. They share a structure (both pay the customer cash directly) and they share a customer (Medicare-eligible seniors). They solve different problems and they apply on different calls. Get the two products clearly separated in your head before you do anything else — the most common new-agent error is treating them as one product.

You'll sell more HHC than HI. The math examples in Section 5 are all HHC. The headline pitch (Rx reimbursement covers most of the premium) is HHC's. HI is the pivot when HHC doesn't fit, and a useful cross-sell on MA calls when the prospect's hospital copay exposure is real. HHC is your main product; HI is the secondary tool — read the doc with that weighting in mind.
⚠ State availability — check first
  • HI Shield is NOT available in Montana or New Mexico. Don't pitch HI in those states.
  • Rx reimbursement is NOT included in Kentucky. The HHC product is still available, but the Rx-net-cost math breaks — pitch HHC on coverage merit only.
  • HHC excluded in several states (most recent confirmed: MT, NV, MD, MN, ND, UT, VA, plus state matrix conflicts). When in doubt, verify with the UNL Advocate before quoting.
  • Idaho HHC: 270-day max benefit (vs 360); 6-month pre-existing limitation.

1A · Hospital Indemnity (HI), in plain English

HI is a small insurance policy that pays the member a fixed amount of cash for each day they're hospitalized. That's the whole product, in one sentence. The structural distinction matters — there are two big categories of insurance, and HI is the less-common one:

CategoryWho gets paidWhat it coversExample
Major medicalThe hospital / provider, directlyThe actual medical billMedicare, Medicare Advantage, Medigap
IndemnityThe member, in cash, on a covered eventWhatever the member needs the cash for — copays, groceries, family helpHospital Indemnity, Home Healthcare
Major medical is a referee that pays the hospital so your prospect doesn't have to. Indemnity is a check that shows up in your prospect's mailbox when a bad thing happens. Two different systems, two different jobs. A prospect can — and many do — have both.

Common HI amounts on UNL plans: $100–$750/day, paid for 3–12 days/year (the member picks). Two variants exist: Guaranteed Issue HI Shield (ages 50–85, no health questions, $100–$450/day) and HI Shield 2.0 (ages 64–85, medically underwritten, $100–$750/day). Most reasonably healthy prospects qualify for at least one.

⛔ HI Shield 2.0 — knockout screen
Ask before pitching HI Shield 2.0. Any "Yes" → not eligible.
  1. In the past 24 months, has the prospect been treated for, prescribed medication for, or diagnosed with any of: cancer (excluding basal cell skin), heart attack, CHF, stroke, TIA, peripheral vascular disease, heart/bypass surgery, defibrillator · insulin-dependent diabetes, Crohn's, ulcerative colitis, liver/pancreas/kidney disease, organ transplant, disease-related amputation · COPD, emphysema, oxygen use · Alzheimer's, dementia, memory-loss meds, Parkinson's, ALS, MS, muscular dystrophy, paralysis · alcohol/substance abuse including opioid addiction · bipolar, schizophrenia, manic or major depression?
  2. Currently uses a wheelchair, OR needs help with ADLs (bathing, dressing, eating, toileting, transferring, taking medications)?
  3. Currently confined to a hospital, ALF, nursing home, psychiatric or correctional facility, or receiving home health / hospice services?
  4. Ever diagnosed or treated for AIDS, ARC, or HIV?
  5. In the past 24 months: advised that a diagnostic test was needed or had abnormal results; hospitalized; had surgery recommended/scheduled/performed; or used tobacco/nicotine?
Any "Yes" on questions 1–5 disqualifies the prospect from HI Shield 2.0. Pivot to GI HI Shield (ages 50–85, no health Q's, $100–$450/day) or to HHC + Rx — both have looser underwriting. Don't lose the sale because one knockout fired.

Critical: oral diabetes (Metformin, etc.) and non-insulin GLP-1s (Trulicity, Ozempic, Mounjaro) are NOT knockouts. Q1b says "diabetes requiring insulin." Don't lose a qualified prospect by misreading this.

Pivot line — HI knocked out (verbatim): "Yeah, the hospital indemnity piece isn't going to fit because of [their condition]. But there's another side of this we should look at — Home Healthcare. Different underwriting, fewer questions, and it has a prescription benefit on top. Want me to walk you through that?"
"Your Medicare Advantage plan is great, but most plans leave a $200–$400 daily copay for the first week of any hospital stay. Hospital Indemnity pays you cash to cover that gap, so a hospital admission doesn't wreck your budget."

1B · Home Healthcare with TCare (HHC), in plain English

HHC is a small insurance policy that helps the member pay for care at home — and pays cash to the family member who actually does the caregiving. Same family as HI (both are indemnity), different trigger.

HHC bundles four benefits in one policy. You pitch all four:

  1. 1. Daily home-care benefits. Cash paid for skilled nursing, therapies, and home health aides when triggered. Three plan tiers (see table below), paid across up to 360 days (270 in Idaho).
  2. 2. The $3,500 caregiver lump sum. Paid directly to a family member (or other person) providing day-to-day care. Up to 2× per lifetime ($7,000 max). The most operationally distinctive feature in the entire ancillary market — no competitor pays a family caregiver this way.
  3. 3. Prescription reimbursement. $10 back per generic fill, $25 back per brand fill, capped at $300 / $600 / $900 per year by plan tier. Submit receipts to claims@unlinsurance.com. Kentucky excluded.
  4. 4. TCare. Care assessment, written plan, vetted referrals, 3-month follow-up. Not a benefit payment — it's the support layer that makes the rest of the policy more useful than competitors'.

★ The HHC trigger — memorize this

A licensed practitioner must certify the insured has EITHER cognitive impairment OR the inability to perform 2 of 6 ADLs — bathing · continence · dressing · eating · toileting · transferring.

That's the door. No homebound test, no prior-hospitalization requirement, no skilled-care need. The trigger is precisely the threshold Medicare won't pay on (see Section 2). Every HHC claim ever paid started here. Issue ages: 55–85.

⛔ HHC — knockout screen
Ask before pitching HHC. Any "Yes" → not eligible.
  1. Currently living in a nursing home or assisted living facility, OR receiving home healthcare / similar care?
  2. Requires assistance or supervision from another person (or device) for any of the 6 ADLs — bathing, continence, dressing, eating, toileting, transferring?
  3. In the past 12 months: diagnosed with Alzheimer's, dementia, or memory loss; prescribed medications for these; or received medical advice for these?
  4. Plan C only — in the next 60 days: scheduled for or anticipating hospital admission, nursing home / ALF stay, home healthcare services, OR surgery?
Plans A & B: any "Yes" on 1–3 = decline. Plan C: any "Yes" on 1–4 = decline. If knocked out of HHC, the prospect may still qualify for HI — pivot. No build chart on HHC. Pre-existing limitation applies (6 months in ID).
Pivot line — HHC knocked out (verbatim): "Got it — the home healthcare side isn't going to be the right fit because of [their answer]. But there's another piece of this — Hospital Indemnity, which pays you cash if you're ever admitted. Different underwriting on that one. Want me to walk you through it?"
"About 70% of people over 65 will need help at home — and Medicare only covers a sliver of it. Home Healthcare pays you cash for that care AND pays $3,500 directly to whoever's helping you. Plus a prescription reimbursement that pays $10/generic and $25/brand on every fill — regardless of what your plan covers — which usually offsets most of the premium."

1C · Frame discipline (the most important paragraph in this doc)

Read it twice. Internalize it before you ever pick up a phone.

HHC is the PRODUCT. Rx reimbursement is the FUNDING MECHANISM.

The customer is buying home healthcare coverage. The prescription benefit pays a fixed amount per fill — $10 per generic, $25 per brand, regardless of what the customer pays at the pharmacy — capped at $300 / $600 / $900 per year by tier. Even a customer whose MA plan covers their prescriptions at $0 copay still gets the full reimbursement. It does not reduce what the customer spends on prescriptions; it pays them on every fill, period. That cash either offsets the premium or — for $0-copay customers — comes back as pure-cash income on top.

This frame discipline is the difference between a defensible sale and a refund-request waiting to happen. Watch the contrast:

❌ Bait-and-switch frame (don't use)✅ Honest frame (use this)
"It's a prescription savings program with home healthcare attached.""It's home healthcare insurance with a built-in prescription benefit."
"You'll save money on your prescriptions.""The prescription benefit covers most of the premium back."
"We pay for your prescriptions.""We pay you $10/generic and $25/brand per fill — regardless of what your plan or pharmacy charges — up to your tier's annual cap."

The bait-and-switch frame fails in two ways. First, a prospect with no prescriptions has nothing to buy — you've sold air. Second, a prospect who does have prescriptions still pays the pharmacy the same amount; if you implied otherwise, that's a refund call when the first month's bill lands.

The corollary: the best customer has BOTH — a real reason to want HHC coverage (felt family burden, cost-of-care concern, preference for staying home) AND prescriptions that fund the math. HHC need without prescriptions = product fit at full premium. Prescriptions without HHC need = customer won't buy what they don't see value in. Both together = the close.

Three things to never say (compliance guardrails)

A compliance mistake doesn't just create a regulatory problem — it erodes trust the moment the prospect realizes the math doesn't work the way you said it did. Three forbidden frames, with the right alternative:

❌ Never sayWhy it's a problem✅ Say this instead
"This is long-term care insurance" / "LTC plan" UNL HHC is a short-term indemnity policy (max 360 days; 270 in ID). Long-term care insurance is a different regulated category with separate disclosure requirements. State DOI exposure. "This is a supplemental indemnity policy that pays cash benefits during a period of home care."
"You'll save money on prescriptions" / "prescription savings program" The Rx benefit reimburses what the prospect already pays — it does not reduce the price of any prescription. Net out-of-pocket goes UP, not down. Misframing is a refund-request waiting to happen. "The plan reimburses $10/generic and $25/brand per fill, up to your tier's annual cap. For a member with regular prescriptions, that offsets most of the premium."
"This replaces Medicare" / "better than Medicare" Illegal framing. UNL HHC is supplemental — never a Medicare replacement. UNL canonical compliance language exists for exactly this reason. "This is a supplemental policy. Benefits are paid as outlined in the policy. This does not change or replace your Medicare coverage."

Next, Section 2: why this product exists at all — the structural gap in Medicare's coverage that explains every objection you'll hear.

Section 2

The Medicare gap — why this product exists

The single most useful thing to internalize about Medicare home care: the gap is a CATEGORY, not a TIME LIMIT.

Medicare covers skilled care indefinitely (no day cap, no visit cap when criteria are met, per 42 CFR § 409.48). It never covers custodial care — bathing, dressing, meals, 24-hour help — when that's the only care needed. The line is drawn on the type of help, not the length of help.

Memorize this image. It's the whole picture in two columns:

                    MEDICAL care            DAILY-LIVING care
                    (skilled, recovery)     (custodial, ongoing)
                    ───────────────────     ───────────────────
Original Medicare      ✅ COVERED              ❌ NOT COVERED
Medicare + Medigap     ✅ COVERED              ❌ NOT COVERED
Medicare Advantage     ✅ COVERED              ❌ NOT COVERED (~93% of plans)
UNL HHC Shield         ✅ Pays cash too        ✅ COVERED
                                                ↑
                                       THE GAP UNL FILLS

Two columns. That's the whole story.

The four facts behind the picture

#FactWhy it matters on a call
1 Medicare home health has NO day cap and NO visit cap. Care can be recertified every 60 days, indefinitely. The famous "100-day rule" people cite is the Skilled Nursing Facility benefit (facility-based, post-hospital), not home health. They're confusing two different benefits.
2 Medicare home health is $0 cost-share when covered — but only when ALL 7 conditions hold. Skilled need + homebound + intermittent (<8 hrs/day, <28 hrs/week) + Medicare-certified agency + (MA only) in-network + prior auth + active plan of care. Miss one → patient pays 100%. "$0 when authorized" sounds unlimited. It's a narrow slice. The slice doesn't include daily-living help at all.
3 Medigap is doubly useless for home care. (a) Medicare home health is already $0 to the patient — there's no cost-share for Medigap to fill. (b) Medigap can't add new benefit categories — if Medicare doesn't cover something, neither does Medigap. Closes the "I have a supplement so I'm covered" objection cleanly without dismissing what Medigap does well elsewhere.
4 Only ~7% of MA plans add real in-home support; ~25% of SNPs do. Per KFF Medicare Advantage 2026 Spotlight. Verified across 9 actual 2026 MA plan SOBs (Humana, Aetna, UHC, Devoted) — only 1 of 9 had real in-home support, and that was a D-SNP inside a $111/mo flex pool shared with OTC + food + utilities. When a prospect says "my MA plan covers home care," the default assumption is wrong. Common false-positives: Resources for Living (referral only), Healthy Options Allowance (flex card), post-discharge meals (short-term recovery only), Wellness Bucks (fitness only). None of those pay someone to bathe a prospect.

The scale of the underlying need

Two HHS ASPE numbers worth holding in your head: ~70% of adults reaching 65 will develop a serious need for extended help at home; ~20% will need that care for 5+ years. Median full-time home health aide cost: ~$77,792/year (Genworth/CareScout 2024). Medicare pays $0 of that when the need is custodial. The math is brutal; the timing is uncertain.

"Medicare, Medigap, and Medicare Advantage all cover medical home care nearly identically. None of them cover daily-living help. UNL Home Healthcare fills that gap."

Section 3

The three call archetypes + verbatim openers

The opener either lowers the prospect's defenses or raises them. The single most common new-agent error — naming the product before earning the right to present it — costs you the sale in the first 15 seconds. The right opener depends on how the call started, so first identify which of three archetypes you're on:

ArchetypeWhenMental stateWhat they knowHI fair game?
Cross-sell pivot Right after you've completed an MA enrollment Open, trusting you, already on the call Their MA plan; trust is pre-built Yes — the MA copay is a real, named exposure
General benefit inbound Inbound from aggregated senior-benefits leads Confused, possibly defensive, possibly hostile Almost nothing; clicked a generic "Medicare benefits" ad No — pivoting to HI without a hospital-pain anchor IS the bait-and-switch
Standalone HHC lead Inbound or outbound on a specific HHC or Rx ad Curious but guarded; been pitched before Responded to a specific HHC or "prescription benefit" ad No — unless hospital pain surfaces organically in Stage 2

Match the opener to the archetype every time. The wrong opener triggers instant defensiveness ("why is this guy so eager?" / "another vendor") and you've lost Stage 2 before it started.

Opener — Cross-sell pivot

"Now [Mr/Mrs Last Name]… before we wrap up, a lot of our members in your situation ask me about a supplemental option that helps with the costs your Medicare Advantage plan still leaves you exposed to. Would it be okay if I shared how that works for a couple minutes?"

This is the Status Frame: pre-positions you as the trusted source ("a lot of our members ask me about…") and asks permission before naming a new product. The "would it be okay if I shared" is the permission gate that earns Stage 2.

Opener — General benefit inbound

"Hi [first name], thanks for reaching out about Medicare benefits. I'm [agent first name] with Clear Path Coverage. My job's pretty simple — I'll ask you a couple of quick questions to see if there's a benefit here you'd qualify for. If there is, I'll walk you through it; if not, I'll just tell you straight. Sound fair?"

Three things this opener does NOT do, on purpose: name a product, quote a price, claim authority. It establishes you as a matcher, not a seller, and gives the prospect a filter promise they can audit. "My job's pretty simple" is a tonality cue (low-energy, neutral) that calms a defensive prospect.

Opener — Standalone HHC lead

"Hi [first name], thanks for calling — name's [agent first name]. I see you reached out about the home healthcare plan with the built-in prescription benefit. I help folks figure out if there's a fit, and if it's not, I'll just tell you. Want to walk through your situation for a couple minutes and see?"

The product is named (the prospect already knows what they responded to) and immediately followed by a filter ("if it's not a fit, I'll tell you"). Trust comes from the willingness to disqualify, not from enthusiasm.

The load-bearing line: "if it's not a fit, I'll tell you straight"

This single phrase is the single most important move on standalone and general-benefit-inbound openers. It distinguishes legitimate brokers from scam vendors in the senior's mental model. Your prospect has been called by 4–6 vendors today; every one sounded eager. You sound willing to walk away. That's the trust differential. Cross-sell calls don't need it (trust is pre-built); the other two live or die by it.

General benefit inbound — the "I never reached out" recovery

A meaningful share of general benefit inbound prospects will deny signing up — they clicked an ad, forgot, conflated you with another vendor. Don't gaslight them. Acknowledge honestly, reset the time budget, reset the filter promise:

"Oh, fair — your information was passed to us by a Medicare benefits service. Sometimes folks click on something and don't think much of it. Either way, since you've got a moment — is it okay if I ask a couple quick questions to see if there's anything here that fits? Two minutes max, and if there's nothing, I'll get out of your hair."

Acknowledge → reset time budget → reset filter → ask permission. Most prospects who'd otherwise hang up will give you the two minutes.

Next, Section 4: what happens between the opener and the application — the NEPQ stage model and the single question that does most of the work.

Section 4

NEPQ in 5 stages + the Preference Question

NEPQ is a sales method — how to run the conversation. It's not new product knowledge. You're going to read about five stages, a Preference Question, looping discipline, and a four-question close sequence. Don't try to memorize all of it on first read. Pick up the three highest-leverage moves — the Preference Question (later in this section), the Stage 3 Transition formula, and the Stage 5 close — and run a few calls. Come back for the rest after call 5. NEPQ is a skill that lands with reps, not a script.

NEPQ in one sentence: Questions surface emotion; statements surface defenses. The "best" choice at any stage almost always ends in a question mark and contains no factual statement the prospect could disagree with. If you find yourself stating a fact, restructure it as a question and let the prospect articulate the answer.

The model is structured around a different time budget than most agents are used to:

The old model (AIDA)NEPQ
~30% closing + ~50% presenting + ~20% rapport / discovery85% engagement (questions + listening) + 10% presentation + 5% commitment

If a 10-minute call has more than ~1 minute of agent monologue, you've slipped out of NEPQ. Every word in your Stage 4 presentation should reference something the prospect said in Stage 2 — not a generic product fact you brought to the call.

Stage 2 — the substance (and the triple-duty discipline)

Stage 2 is 85% of the call. Every Stage 2 question should do three jobs at once: data (a fact you'll need later — knockout check, math input, presentation reference), rapport (show genuine interest), and seed (plant a future need by getting the prospect to articulate something).

If a question only does one job, it's an interrogation, small talk, or a leading question. Triple-duty is what separates Stage 2 from a survey.

The Preference Question — the highest-leverage move on the call

After the first few Situation questions, when you understand their living situation and family nearby, ask this exact question:

"If you ever needed care, would you rather stay home or go into a facility?"

About 95% of seniors say "home." That single answer does three things at once:

  1. Data — confirms home preference, which is the HHC sale
  2. Seed — they've articulated the value proposition before you've named the product
  3. Anchor — in Stage 4, you link back: "You said you'd want to stay home — that's exactly what this is designed for."

This is the lever for general benefit inbound calls. The prospect doesn't know they want HHC when the call starts. The Preference Question is what makes them articulate it. Once they've said "home" out loud, the Stage 4 presentation pivots into something they asked for, not something you sold.

What this sounds like on a real call — 60-second sample exchange

Imagine you've just finished an MA enrollment with John, 67, takes 4 generics monthly. Here's how the next 60 seconds of an ancillary cross-sell sound when you use the moves above:

You: "Now John, before we wrap up — a lot of our members ask me about a supplemental option that helps with the costs your MA plan still leaves you exposed to. Would it be okay if I shared how that works for a couple minutes?"

John: Yeah, sure.

You: "Tell me about your current prescriptions — how many do you fill regularly? Mostly generics or brand names? How often — monthly, quarterly?"

John: Four. All generics. Monthly.

You: "Got it. And quick one — if you ever needed care, would you rather stay home or go into a facility?"

John: Home, obviously.

You: "Say more about that — what makes 'home' the answer?" [looping]

John: I just don't want to end up in some nursing home with strangers. My wife's here, my kids are nearby.

You: "Yeah, that makes sense. Let me ask — Medicare home care has a real gap, and most folks don't realize it. If you ended up needing help at home for several months — bathing, dressing, that kind of help — what would you do?"

John: Honestly, I haven't thought about it. I figured Medicare would cover it.

You: "That's the most common assumption — and it's actually the gap. Medicare covers the medical kind of home care, but not the daily-living kind. So here's what we could do for you…"

Notice: no facts stated. Every line is a question or a loop. The agent has surfaced John's preference (home), his felt concern (burden on family), and the Medicare gap — without ever pitching a product. By the time the Stage 4 presentation lands, John is leaning in, not bracing.

Looping doctrine — the most under-practiced NEPQ move

When the prospect says something important, don't acknowledge with "right" or "I see." Loop it back:

"You said [their exact phrase] — say more about that?"

Looping doubles trust (the prospect feels heard), forces elaboration (the second articulation is more emotional and specific than the first), surfaces the real concern under the surface concern, and buys you thinking time without sounding scripted.

NEPQ benchmark: 6–12 looping moves per call. Gone 90 seconds without one? You're missing rapport.

Tonality discipline

Tonality is half the call. Same script delivered fast and salesy fails; delivered calm and curious, it lands.

  • • Calm, curious, slightly slower than feels natural — Stages 1–3
  • • Skeptical / concerned when probing problems — Stage 2B
  • • Conversational, not pitchy — Stage 4
  • • Soft, almost casual on the close — Stage 5

Senior calls especially: pace below the prospect's, never above. Eager + fast + loud is hangup bait — they've been trained by years of scam vendors to hang up on that signal.

Stage 3 — the Transition formula

Once Stage 2 has surfaced problems + future state + consequences, transition with:

"Based on what you told me… what we're doing could actually work for you…

Because you know how you said… [repeat their words — e.g., 'you'd want to stay home if anything ever came up and not be a burden on your daughter']…

And because of that, it's making you feel… [repeat the emotion — frustrated, stressed, exposed, worried]…

With your permission, I can go over a couple things we could do for you to help with your situation. Would that help?"

The prospect can't disagree with their own words. By the time you say "would that help?", the answer is already implicitly yes.

Q2 is the non-obvious move most agents miss. They hear "yes" and rush to paperwork. NEPQ says: make them defend the yes. By the time they've finished explaining why this fits them, in their own words, they've sold themselves harder than you ever could.

Next, Section 5: the math that makes the close work — and the three personas that show how to apply it.

Section 5

The math reframe + three worked examples

HHC is the product. Rx is the funding mechanism. Net out-of-pocket goes UP slightly compared to not having the plan at all. Not down. The Rx benefit returns what the prospect is already spending — up to a tier cap — and that returned money funds the policy premium. If you frame it as "savings on prescriptions," you've set up the bait-and-switch we're trying to avoid.

The formula

Annual premium − Annual Rx reimbursement received = Annual net cost

Annual net cost ÷ 12 = Real monthly out-of-pocket

The reimbursement is $10 per generic fill, $25 per brand fill, capped at $300 / $600 / $900 per year by plan tier. Reimbursement is calculated per fill, not per month — a prescription filled quarterly counts as 4 fills/year, not 12.

A prospect on 4 generics filled monthly = 48 fills/yr × $10 = $480/yr potential. Same prospect on 90-day fills = 16 fills × $10 = $160/yr. 3× difference from the same medication list, just on cadence. This is why the Stage 2A Situation question always includes "How often are you filling them — monthly, quarterly?" — same med count + different cadence can be the difference between a Plan A fit and a Plan A misfit.

Two verbatim moves for the live call

Before quoting any premium — the math pre-frame. This mitigates sticker shock when the gross premium lands:

"Bear with me — I'm going to give you the gross premium first, and then the math that actually matters."

The fixed-income senior's brain hears "fifty dollars" before "five dollars" lands. The pre-frame gives them permission to absorb the big number without flinching, knowing better math is coming.

The Rx-net-cost reframe — handles the price objection before it surfaces. Use this in Stage 4, after establishing Rx burden:

"Let me ask you this — if I told you we could get you a plan where the prescription reimbursement covers most of your premium — meaning you're paying maybe $3–$5 a month out of pocket after the Rx benefit kicks in — AND you're getting home healthcare coverage on top — how would that look to you?"

Then immediately: "Does that make sense as how it works?"

This single question pattern handles "sounds expensive" before the prospect ever says it. The product now reads as paying for itself rather than as a new monthly expense.

Next, Section 6: the five most common objections you'll hear, with verbatim responses you can deploy on the next call.

Section 6

The 5 most common objections — verbatim responses

Most HHC objections are education gaps, not genuine resistance. The prospect believes something that isn't true. Your job is to correct the frame without making them feel wrong. Universal pattern:

Acknowledge → reframe with a question → continue discovery. Never argue, never explain unprompted.

Print this section. Memorize. Use verbatim until it sounds like your own voice.

1. "Doesn't Medicare cover the first 100 days of home care?"

"That's a really common mix-up — the 100-day rule is for Skilled Nursing Facility care after a hospital stay, not home health. Medicare home health actually has no time limit at all — your doctor can recertify your home health coverage every 60 days for as long as you qualify. The catch is what kind of care it covers — skilled care like nursing visits or therapy. It doesn't cover help with bathing, dressing, or 24-hour care when those are the only things you need. That's the gap."
Why it works: "Common mix-up" corrects without making them feel stupid. Acknowledging the 100-day rule IS real (for SNF) keeps you credible. Closes on the CATEGORY gap — lands exactly where Section 2 wants the conversation.

2. "I have a Medicare Supplement, so I'm covered."

"Your Medigap is great for what it does — it pays the deductibles and coinsurance Medicare leaves behind on hospital and outpatient care. But here's something most people don't realize: Medicare home health is already $0 to you when it's covered. There's no cost-share for Medigap to pay there. Plus, Medigap can't add new benefit categories — so personal care that Medicare doesn't cover, Medigap doesn't either. That's the gap this product fills."
Why it works: Both structural reasons Medigap can't help — (a) no cost-share to fill, (b) can't expand categories — close the objection completely. Honoring what Medigap does well keeps the prospect from feeling their coverage is being attacked.

3. "My Medicare Advantage plan has supplemental benefits."

"Some MA plans do, but it's only about 7% of individual plans in 2026 — more for the special needs plans. And when they do, it's usually capped at small amounts and limited to chronically ill members. Worth checking your plan's Summary of Benefits — I can walk through it with you if you want. The home care coverage we're discussing pays you cash directly, regardless of what your plan does."
Why it works: 7% is verifiable (KFF 2026 Spotlight). Offering to walk their actual SOB converts the objection into a discovery moment. If their plan genuinely does have real in-home support, acknowledge it honestly and disqualify gracefully (see Section 7).

4. "I'm in good shape, I won't need this."

"Honestly, that's the right time to set this up. About 70% of adults reaching 65 develop a serious need for help at home, and 20% need care for 5+ years. The thing is, you can only buy this kind of coverage when you're healthy — the underwriting gets stricter once a health change happens, so locking it in now keeps your options open."
Why it works: "That's the right time" converts objection into reason-to-act. HHS ASPE stat (70% lifetime need for extended help at home) is defensible — phrased as "help at home" instead of "long-term care" to avoid the compliance frame trap (per Section 7). The closer is structurally true: HHC has 3 application knockouts (4 for Plan C); a single health event often triggers one.

5. "Sounds expensive."

"Yeah, fair — quick: when you say expensive, what's the number you have in your head?" [pause for answer] "Got it. Let me show you what the math actually looks like for your situation. You mentioned [their Rx count, e.g., 'three or four prescriptions']. The reimbursement pays $10 a generic, $25 a brand — every fill, regardless of what your plan charges. So even if your prescriptions are $0 copay through your plan, you're still getting roughly $50 a month back. Plan B is around $53 a month. So your real out-of-pocket ends up around $3–$5 a month — for $100,000 in home healthcare coverage and a $3,500 payout to whoever ends up taking care of you. Does that look different than what you were expecting?"
Why it works: Never defend a price without first asking the number in their head. Pulling the Rx count they gave you in Stage 2 proves you were listening, not running a script. The closing question invites them back into conversation rather than closing them.

Next, Section 7: the three things you can never say, the disqualification line that protects your close rate, and the deeper resources to use once you've started running calls.

Section 7

Compliance, graceful disqualification, and where to go for more

Graceful disqualification (the move that protects your close rate)

The three compliance guardrails (never say LTC, never say "save on Rx," never say "replaces Medicare") are introduced in Section 1C · Frame discipline. Reread that table whenever you're tempted to soften a frame for a hesitant prospect.

When the math doesn't work, say so. Verbatim:

"Honestly, if you're not filling 4 or more prescriptions regularly — even free ones through your plan — this benefit isn't going to make the math work in your favor. I'm not going to waste your time pretending it does. Is there anything else Medicare-related I could point you toward?"

The economics favor disciplined disqualification. ~3 minutes to disqualify cleanly vs. ~10–12 minutes pushing a non-fit. An agent who handles 20 leads/day with this discipline closes more total sales than one who burns half the day on non-fits — saved time goes to the 6–8 leads that actually fit. Disqualification = throughput = revenue. Wear it as a badge.

If any HHC application knockout is YES — currently in nursing home / ALF / receiving home care; needs ADL help; Alzheimer's / dementia / memory medication in last 12 months (Plan C adds: scheduled admission / surgery / home care next 60 days) — do not submit the application. Details in Academy Section 1.5.

Two UNL mantras

Cash clarity beats feature overload. "$300 a day in your pocket while you're admitted" lands; "Daily Hospital Confinement Indemnity Benefit" doesn't.
You are not selling fear. You are offering options, clarity, and control. UNL's published telesales final mindset. Surface the gap; never weaponize it.

Section 8

Cross-sell mastery — the three sales journeys

Three calls a day, three different journeys, one method underneath all of them. By now you have the foundation — products, the Medicare gap, the openers, NEPQ, the math, the objections, the guardrails. This section assembles all of that into the three sales sequences you'll actually run on real calls. Master these three patterns and you're handling about 95% of the calls that come through your headset.

The key insight for a new agent: the NEPQ method doesn't change between journeys. What changes is (a) the opener, (b) the specific pivot move that bridges between products, and (c) where the math comes in. Read each journey looking for the pivot — that's where most new agents stumble and where the experienced ones make their money.

Journey 1 · Most common

MA-First (Inbound)

Your bread-and-butter call. SMID-approved Medicare lead. Verbal SOA at start covers MA + HI + HHC.

The setup: Customer responded to a SMID-approved MA ad and called us. They're consumer-initiated, which means the 48-hour SOA waiting period doesn't apply. After the TPMO disclaimer, you capture a verbal SOA that enumerates every product category you might discuss — MA, MAPD, PDP, HI, HHC. Customer says Yes/No to each. You proceed.

What happens next depends on the MA outcome. There are two paths from here, and the difference between them is where most agents lose money.

Path 1a · MA enrollment closes
After the MA close, transition to ancillary

You completed the MA enrollment. Customer is satisfied. Trust is pre-built — they just enrolled with you. The cross-sell bridge uses what you already captured on the SOA:

"Great — you're enrolled and you'll get your card in about 7–10 days. Last thing before we wrap up: we agreed at the start to also discuss Hospital Indemnity and Home Healthcare. Your new plan has a $300/day copay if you get admitted, and Medicare itself doesn't cover daily-living help at home. Couple quick questions to see if either is a fit?"

From here, NEPQ Stage 2A questions: prescription count, fill cadence, family situation, ADL independence. Then the Preference Question from Section 4 — "if you ever needed care, would you rather stay home or go into a facility?" — to surface the HHC anchor. Then Stage 2B Problem Awareness on hospital exposure and the home care gap. Then the math reframe from Section 5.

Treat the cross-sell as "completing their coverage," not "selling them more." The customer trusted you with their MA enrollment — they will extend that trust to HI/HHC if you frame the gaps honestly. Lead with the Rx math because it makes the entire package look essentially self-funded.

Path 1b · MA doesn't close · the expert move
When you can't help with Medicare, you can still help

Sometimes MA doesn't work out. The customer isn't in a valid SEP. Their current plan is actually a good fit. The plan they want isn't available in their county. They decide they want to keep their plan. They're not yet 65. Most new agents end the call here. The pros pivot to the ancillary rescue.

Even if you couldn't get a customer enrolled in a new MA plan today, two things are still true: (1) their current MA plan still has hospital copay exposure, and (2) Medicare doesn't cover daily-living home care for any plan. Both gaps can be filled with HI and HHC — without changing anything about their current Medicare. This one pivot turns dead calls into incremental income. Over 50 calls, the difference between agents who use this move and agents who don't is dramatic.

The pivot script (verbatim):

"Look — even though we can't change your MA plan today, your current plan still leaves you with the daily hospital copay if you ever get admitted, and Medicare doesn't cover daily-living help at home regardless of which plan you're on. There's separate coverage that helps with both of those — it works alongside your current Medicare and doesn't affect it at all. Couple quick questions to see if it's a fit?"

Three reframes to keep in your back pocket for this path:

  • • HI angle: "Your current MA plan leaves a daily copay if you're admitted. HI pays you cash directly — doesn't matter what carrier your Medicare is with."
  • • HHC angle: "This gap is structural — Medicare covers skilled care, not daily-living help. Doesn't matter which MA plan you have. HHC pays cash for that home care, plus $3,500 directly to whoever ends up taking care of you."
  • • Rx angle: "If you take prescriptions, the reimbursement covers most of the premium back. For 4 medications, you're looking at $3–5/month real cost — for $100,000 in home healthcare coverage."

From here, NEPQ proceeds the same as Path 1a. The customer doesn't realize they have ongoing exposure — your job is to surface it with questions, then offer the solution. Same Preference Question, same math reframe, same close moves.

Sample exchange — Path 1b in action

Agent: "...so unfortunately you're not in a valid enrollment period right now, so I can't change your plan today. But here's the thing — your current plan still has a $300/day hospital copay if you ever get admitted, and Medicare doesn't cover daily-living help at home for any plan. There's separate coverage that helps with both. Couple quick questions?"

Customer: Yeah, sure.

Agent: "Tell me about your medications — how many do you fill monthly?"

Customer: Four — mostly generics, one brand.

Agent: "Got it. And if you ever needed care, would you rather stay home or go into a facility?"

Customer: Home, definitely.

Agent: "Yeah, most folks say that. And one more — if you got hospitalized for 5 days, that's $1,500 in copays under your current plan. What would $1,500 in surprise bills do to your situation?"

Customer: That'd be tight. We don't have much cushion right now.

Agent: "I hear you. Here's what we could do for you..." [pivot to Stage 4 math reframe + presentation]

By the time the agent reaches the math, the customer has articulated three things: home preference, family financial vulnerability, prescription cost. The Stage 4 presentation is no longer a pitch — it's the answer to questions the customer just asked themselves.

Journey 2

Ancillary-First (Inbound)

Non-Medicare lead. Lead with HHC. Route MA to a separate consumer-initiated call.

The setup: Customer responded to an HHC-specific or Rx-reimbursement creative. They're not a Medicare lead — they came in because of the home-care or prescription angle specifically. Compliance rule: never discuss MA on this call. If the customer raises it, route to a separate inbound MA call they initiate.

This journey is the foundation already covered in Section 3 (opener), Section 4 (NEPQ method), Section 5 (math), and Section 6 (objections). Read those sections cold before running this journey live.

The one move unique to this journey: the MA handoff. If the customer asks about Medicare Advantage mid-call:

"That's a great question — yes, we can absolutely help you with Medicare Advantage. But by the rules I need to ask you to give us a separate call to do that properly. Our Medicare team handles it on a different line — call us back at [MA inbound number] and just tell them you were talking to me on the home healthcare side. They'll take great care of you.

Now, while I have you — want to keep going on the Home Healthcare piece we were on?"

This works because it (a) honors their interest, (b) keeps you compliant, (c) preserves the active conversation. Document the MA referral in EnrollHere as a separate event with the inbound number you gave them.

If hospital pain surfaces organically in your Stage 2 discovery — they mention a recent admission, family member's hospital scare, or budget anxiety about copays — you can pitch HI on the same call. HHC and HI are both ancillary; both can be on the same ancillary call. The MA discipline is specifically about Medicare Advantage, not hospital indemnity.

Journey 3 · High-intent

HHC Outbound (PTC, High-Intent)

Pre-qualified PTC-documented leads — customers who opted in, take prescriptions, passed knockouts. Conversion rates are materially higher than inbound general-benefit.

The setup: Customer filled out a form, called a number, or otherwise opted in for information about HHC. They've already been pre-qualified — they take prescriptions (so the Rx math works) and passed the knockout screen (so they're underwriting-eligible). You have documented Permission to Contact. This is a pure ancillary outbound — MCMG doesn't attach unless MA enters the conversation.

The opener is different from inbound. You're calling them, not the other way around — so the tone has to be calm, low-pressure, matter-of-fact. Acknowledge that they may not remember signing up. Filter-promise hard.

"Hi [first name], this is [agent] with Clear Path Coverage. I'm following up — you reached out to us about a week ago asking about the home healthcare plan with the prescription reimbursement. Just wanted to follow up and see if you've still got questions. Is now a good time?"

Why this opener works:

  • • Names the specific product they opted in for — proves you're the right person, not a random vendor
  • • Acknowledges they may have forgotten — "a week ago" prompts memory without putting them on the spot
  • • Low-pressure close — "is now a good time?" gives them an easy out, which paradoxically reduces resistance
  • • No premature pitch — no claim, no price, no benefits mentioned until they've consented to the conversation

If they say "I don't remember signing up":

"Oh, fair — your information was passed to us through a senior benefits service you reached out to. Sometimes folks click on something and don't think much of it. Either way, you've got a minute — is it okay if I ask a couple quick questions to see if there's anything here that fits for you? If it's not a fit, I'll tell you straight."

If they consent, the discovery is faster than inbound general-benefit because they're pre-qualified. The math works for most of them. Don't drag it out:

  1. Stage 2A · Confirm fill cadence — "Just confirming what we have — you're filling [N] prescriptions [monthly/quarterly]?" One sentence, gets the math input.
  2. Stage 2C · Preference Question — "If you ever needed care, would you rather stay home or go into a facility?" 95% say home. That's your anchor.
  3. Stage 2B · Problem Awareness — "If you needed help at home for a few months, who'd be helping you?" Surfaces family burden in one question.
  4. Stage 4 · Math reframe — Go straight to "based on the meds you mentioned, your real cost looks like $3–5 a month for $100,000 in coverage...". High-intent leads can handle the math early.
  5. Stage 5 · Commitment sequence — Q1 ("do you feel this could be the answer?") → Q2 ("why do you feel it is?") → application transition.

Per-call expectation: 8–12 minutes total. Discovery is shorter; math comes in earlier; close is faster. The customer was already most of the way to "yes" when you dialed — your job is to confirm fit and walk them through the application.

If a high-intent outbound customer asks about MA: same handoff script as Journey 2. Route to a separate inbound MA call they initiate. Don't get pulled into MA on an HHC-scoped outbound call.

The five master principles — what unifies all three journeys

Three openers, three pivot moves, one underlying method. The patterns that show up in every journey:

  1. 1. The verbal SOA at call start covers ALL products you might discuss. Belt and suspenders. Capturing HI + HHC on the SOA at the start of every MA call means you never have to stop the call to obtain a new SOA mid-conversation. Same logic for HHC outbound: list all ancillary product categories on the verbal SOA at the start.
  2. 2. The Rx-net-cost reframe is universal. If the prospect takes prescriptions, the math closes. Path 1a, Path 1b, Journey 2, Journey 3 — every fit prospect on every journey gets the same math: "$3–5 a month real cost for $100,000 in coverage and the $3,500 caregiver lump." Memorize it. Run it on every fit.
  3. 3. Knockouts are your friends. They tell you when to STOP pitching. If a knockout fires on HI, pivot to HHC. If a knockout fires on HHC, pivot to HI. If both fail, disqualify gracefully (Section 7). Don't pitch through a knockout — that's how compliance complaints get generated.
  4. 4. The handoff script is the same across journeys. Customer wants something outside scope? "We can absolutely help with that — different call. Here's the number / our [X] team will take great care of you." Don't pivot on the current call. Don't try to fold it in. Route and continue.
  5. 5. Compliance is your shield, not your obstacle. The SOA, the TPMO disclaimer, the recording — these protect you. Following them lets you sell with confidence because you have a defensible record on every call. The compliance playbook (separate doc) has the full rules; Section 7 has the in-doc guardrails.

What "expert" looks like by call 50

After your first 50 calls, expert behavior shows up in three places:

  • • You spot the journey within the first 30 seconds. The lead source, the customer's first sentence, the energy of the call — all signal which journey you're on. You pick the opener accordingly.
  • • You handle the MA rescue without thinking about it. When MA doesn't close, the pivot to ancillary is automatic. You don't end the call defeated — you transition smoothly to "even though we can't change your plan today..."
  • • You disqualify fast. When a prospect doesn't fit, you graceful-DQ in under 3 minutes and move to the next call. Your throughput goes up; your close rate on the fits goes up; your CTM volume stays at zero.

Three journeys. One method. Every call you take is a variation of one of these patterns. Run them enough times and they stop feeling like sales — they start feeling like helping people solve real problems with real coverage they didn't know they could afford.
